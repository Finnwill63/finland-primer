🥇The Single Best Money-Saving Habit in Finland

Eat your main meal at noon. Finnish restaurants serve a weekday lounas — a lunch buffet or set plate, roughly 11:00 to 14:00, for €11–15. That typically includes a hot main or buffet, a salad bar, bread, water and coffee. The same kitchen charges two to three times as much for dinner. University restaurants (UniCafe and equivalents) serve the general public at around €10–13, and serve students cheaper still if you have a Finnish student card.

Students who figure this out in week one eat well all semester. Students who don't spend the same money on worse food at 8pm.

🧭Culture & Etiquette

Do this

  • Say hello and be friendly — keep your volume moderate
  • Give people personal space, even in queues
  • Say yes to a sauna invitation immediately
  • Use the words: kiitos (thanks), hei (hi), anteeksi (sorry)
  • Trust what Finns say — they mean it literally
  • Take your shoes off in someone's home
  • Order the lounas

Don't do this

  • Don't be loud on public transport
  • Don't mistake quiet for coldness — it's respect, not rejection
  • Don't perform enthusiasm — Finns understate; match their register
  • Don't jaywalk — Finns wait for the green, always
  • Don't haggle — prices are fixed
  • Don't take photos in the sauna
  • Don't over-share with strangers — brevity is respected
🤐
The silence thing is real and it is not about you. A Finn who stops talking is thinking, or is comfortable, or has finished. Americans instinctively fill that gap. Resist it for three seconds longer than feels natural and you will find the conversation goes somewhere better.
🔥The Sauna

Finland has 3.3 million saunas for 5.5 million people. This is not a spa amenity — it is a cultural cornerstone, and it is the single fastest way to stop being a foreigner here.

Protocol: shower first, always. Sit on a towel or seat cover. Mixed saunas require swimwear; single-sex traditional saunas are nude and completely non-sexual — keeping a swimsuit on in one marks you out immediately. Löyly is the steam from water thrown on the stones; it's communal, so ask before throwing. Alternate heat, cold and rest in cycles. Quiet conversation is fine; phones are not.

Sompasauna
Free · 24/7 · Kalasatama shore
Volunteer-built, no staff, no showers, no lockers. You chop the wood and stoke it yourself. Mixed, clothing optional. Bring a towel, water and a few coins for the donation box. The most communal thing in the city and a perennial student favourite.
€0
Kotiharjun Sauna
€16 · Tue–Sun 14:00–20:00 · Kallio
Open since 1928 — the last traditional wood-burning public sauna in Helsinki. Separate men's (ground floor) and women's (upstairs), nude. Towel €4, birch vihta €8. No booking, just turn up. Unpolished and completely authentic.

Uusi Sauna

Modern neighbourhood sauna with a bar and bistro attached. Separate wood-heated men's and women's saunas, nude standard. Seat cover included, towel €5. The best value-for-authenticity in the middle of the range, with a local crowd rather than a tourist one.
Allas Sea Pool
Student €14 weekday / €17 weekend · Market Square
Five saunas, a 27°C heated pool and a 15°C sea pool, open year-round right by the harbour. Swimsuit required. Ask for the student price — it's a real discount and easy to miss.

🍽Eat & Drink

The food you should actually try

Karjalanpiirakka — Karelian pie
Rye crust, rice filling, topped with egg butter. Finland's national snack and perfection in simplicity. About €1.50 in any supermarket, better at a market hall. Eat many.
Lohikeitto — salmon soup
Creamy dill salmon chowder with potatoes and leeks. The comfort food of Finland. Order it. Non-negotiable.
Ruisleipä — rye bread
Dense, dark, slightly sour sourdough rye. Finnish superpower food. With butter and cheese, it is breakfast for the entire country.
Korvapuusti — cinnamon bun
Flatter than an American cinnamon roll, cardamom-spiced, and vastly superior to anything Cinnabon has ever produced.

Where to eat it without spending much

Hakaniemi Market Hall
Hakaniemi · Zero tourists
Covered market hall plus an outdoor market. Karelian pies, Finnish textiles, coffee, lunch at local prices. The one to learn first.
Hietalahti Market Hall
Lönnrotinkatu 34 · Weekend flea market
Market food, coffee, fresh fish, Karelian pies. Much less touristy than Market Square, and a great morning stop. The weekend flea market spills out from here — bring a little cash, some stalls are cash-only.
Fafa's
Multiple locations · ~€10
Helsinki's best falafel and pita chain. Fast, cheap, and a genuine local obsession rather than a tourist thing.
Konstan Möljä
Hietalahdenkatu 14 · Dinner ~€22
All-you-can-eat Finnish buffet — sautéed reindeer, Karelian stew, oven-baked salmon, pies, daily soup. A Helsinki institution since 1981, family-run with a maritime soul. The Tue–Fri lunch is the real value. Book ahead for dinner.
Restaurant Sea Horse
Kapteeninkatu 11 · Open since 1933
Helsinki's most legendary Finnish bistro — fried Baltic herring, vorschmack, salmon soup, meatballs, reindeer. Beloved by artists and sailors, nicknamed Sikala, "the Pigsty." Reserve; it fills.
S-Market and K-Market
Everywhere · Your actual food budget
Breakfast, snacks, prepared food. Evening markdowns on prepared items are steep and locals absolutely use them. Note supermarkets sell beer, cider and drinks up to 8% ABV only, and only 09:00–21:00.
🍷
Anything stronger than 8% comes from Alko, the state monopoly — spirits and most wine. Since 4 July 2026 Alko has extended hours and 35 stores open on Sundays, and home delivery is legal. Bring your passport: ID is checked routinely, on people who look well over 30, and US driver's licences are sometimes refused.
🗺Neighborhoods to Know
Kallio
Nightlife · Cheap · Where students actually live
Former working-class district, now the bohemian heart. Dive bars, natural wine, second-hand shops, kebab joints, record stores, Siltanen and Kaiku for electronic music. Kotiharjun Sauna and Hakaniemi Market Hall are both here. Rowdy late on weekends and completely fine. If you get to choose where you live, choose here.
Punavuori / Design District
Design · Galleries · Brunch
Around 200 businesses carry the Design District marking. Boutiques, architecture bookshops, third-wave coffee, the Design Museum, Sinebrychoff park. Best experienced as an aimless Saturday-morning wander rather than a shopping trip.
Töölö
Parks · Running · Museums
Elegant, green and calm. Sibelius Monument, the Olympic Stadium, Töölönlahti bay, Hietaniemi beach, and Café Regatta — a tiny red waterside hut with famous cinnamon buns. Where you go to run, swim in summer, or think.
Jätkäsaari
Modern · Cheap beds · Ferry terminal
Reclaimed harbour land, built out over fifteen years. Glassy, a bit raw, windswept in winter. Cheapest hostels, and this is where the Tallinn ferries leave from (West Terminal 2). Trams 7 and 9 reach the centre in six to nine minutes.
Vallila
Wooden houses · No tourists
Beyond Kallio. Puu-Vallila is a preserved district of painted wooden houses and one of the prettiest, least-visited corners of the city. Konepaja, a converted railway machine works, now holds bars, restaurants and events.
📍Don't Miss
  • Suomenlinna Sea Fortress — UNESCO site built from 1748 across six linked islands, 15 minutes by HSL ferry from Market Square on a normal transit ticket. It has lived under Swedish, Russian and Finnish rule, and about 850 people still live there. Walking the stone walls and gun batteries at 9pm in summer light is an experience you'll remember for years. The island is free; only the ferry is ticketed.
  • Oodi Library — possibly the world's best public library. Modern architecture, free to enter, free to sit in all day, and a genuinely warm refuge in February.
  • Temppeliaukio (Rock Church) — carved into living granite. One of the world's great architectural statements.
  • Kamppi Chapel of Silence — a wooden chapel in the middle of a shopping district. Ten minutes of actual silence in the city. Free, and more affecting than it sounds.
  • Senate Square & Cathedral — neoclassical, and best at magic hour, which in June is somewhere around 11pm.
  • Market Square (Kauppatori) — harbour market for berries, salmon and a morning ritual. Touristy but pleasant early.
  • Free museum windows — the National Museum is free Friday 4–6pm, and several others run similar slots. Worth checking the month you arrive.
🚌Getting Around

From the airport

Trains I and P are the same loop in opposite directions — take whichever comes. The station is directly under the terminal, the ride is 27–32 minutes, and you need an ABC ticket at €4.50 (€4.80 if you tap a card instead of using the app). Bus 600 does the same job in about 45 minutes on the same ticket. Skip the taxi at €45–70; Uber and Bolt run around €25–40 and pick up at the P2 parking hall rather than the arrivals curb.

Around town

  • Trams are the best way to move. Lines 2 and 3 cover most of the centre. A single AB ride is €3.30 in the app.
  • Day tickets start at €10.60 (AB) and pay off at four rides. Multi-day runs 1–7 days from machines, 1–13 days in the app.
  • Contactless tap works on buses, trams and commuter trains, and at metro and ferry readers — but costs €0.20–0.40 more per ride than the app. There are no gates; inspections are real and fines are steep.
  • The HSL app is the one thing to install before you land. Its journey planner tells you exactly which zone ticket a trip needs.
  • City bikes run roughly May to October, app-based, and the terrain is flat.
🎓
If you're here for a semester, ask about an HSL student discount — enrolled students at Finnish institutions get substantially reduced season tickets. It requires registering your student status with HSL, which takes a little paperwork and pays for itself in about three weeks.
🌲Nuuksio National Park — No Car Needed

45 km² of boreal forest and 30+ lakes, about 30 km from the city, and reachable on a normal transit ticket. This surprises everyone.

Step 1 · ~25 min
Commuter train to Espoon keskus
Lines E, L, U, X or Y from Helsinki Central.
Step 2 · ~25 min
Bus 245 from bay 11
Year-round. Bus 245A extends further to Kattila in summer.
Step 3
Get off at Haltia
The Finnish Nature Centre is right at the stop — exhibitions, café, equipment rental. For serious walking, stay on to Punjonsuo and walk 100m to the marked route toward Haukkalampi.

Ticket: Haltia is in zone C, which catches people out. An ABC day ticket at €12.80 covers the round trip plus all your city travel that day.

Trails: Korpin kierros (8 km, 2.5–3 hrs) · Haukankierros (4 km) · Maahisenkierros (2 km, accessible). Marked with colour-coded circles, with free wilderness huts and fire pits along the way.

Everyman's Right (jokamiehenoikeus) means you may walk, forage berries and mushrooms, and wild camp almost anywhere in Finland, for free. There is no park entry fee and no permit. It is one of the genuinely great things about living here.

🦟
Two warnings. Bus service thins out badly in the evening and on Sundays — check the last 245 before you set out, because missing it is a real problem. And from June through August, mosquito repellent is not optional. The mosquitoes here are a serious proposition.
🌙Nightlife
  • Kallio is the answer to almost every "where should we go out" question. Neighbourhood bars, cheap beer, no dress code, no pretension.
  • Doors get busy around 10pm and later than you'd expect. Finns pre-drink at home — supermarket beer before 21:00 is a structural part of the evening.
  • Bring your passport. ID is checked at the door constantly and a US licence is often refused.
  • Siltanen and Kaiku for electronic music, DTM (Iso Roobertinkatu 28) as the main LGBTQ+ venue, Konepaja in Vallila for a Berlin-style outdoor terrace.
  • Drinks are expensive — €7–9 a pint. This is the line item that wrecks student budgets here, not food.
🚀Getting Into the Startup Scene for Almost Nothing

If any part of why you came is Finland's reputation for building companies, this is the most valuable page in the guide. A country of 5.6 million produced Linux, Nokia, Supercell, Wolt, Oura and ICEYE, and the whole scene is unusually open to students — largely because students built most of it.

Volunteer at Slush
Mid-November · Free, and the single best move
Slush was started by Aalto students and is still run by around 1,800 volunteers. Tickets cost €395–1,195 and investor passes require approval — but volunteers get in. It's ~12,000 attendees, 5,800 startups and 3,300 investors over two days. Applications open months ahead and fill; if you're here in autumn, put this in your calendar in September. Nothing else you can do as a student here puts you in the same room as this.
Apply early — slush.org
Maria 01 day pass
€25 · Lapinlahdenkatu 16
A startup campus in a former hospital — around 20,000 m², 200+ member companies, 1,500–2,000 people from 40+ nationalities, and 300+ events a year. A €25 day pass gets you in to cowork. Many of the events are free and open; check their calendar before paying for anything.
Aalto Founder School
Otaniemi · 20 min by metro
The current entry point to entrepreneurship at Aalto, replacing the Aalto Ventures Program. Founder Talks run monthly and are the low-commitment way in. There's also a Founder Minor, an intensive Founder Sprint with roughly a 10% acceptance rate, and a €500–5,000 prototyping and validation grant. If you're on exchange at Aalto or Helsinki, ask what's open to you — cross-registration is often possible.
A Grid and Demo Day
Otaniemi · Spring
25,000 m² of startup hub on the Aalto campus — 160+ startups, a makerspace, the European Space Agency's Finnish business incubator, and UN Technology Innovation Labs. The annual Demo Day drew ~800 visitors, 90 startup booths and 75+ investors in 2026, and is open to walk in.
LUMI AI Factory Hub
Kumpula campus · Free
One of the EU's first AI Factories, opened in the Exactum building in December 2025. AI research-software-engineering support, consultation, training and hackathons, free of charge to students, researchers, startups and SMEs, with access to LUMI supercomputer capacity. If you're doing anything computational, this is an unusually open piece of infrastructure and almost nobody outside Finland knows it exists.
💡
One piece of context that will make you sound informed rather than touristic. Helsinki became a startup city partly by accident: Nokia's collapse from 2011 released thousands of engineers at once, with severance and a Nokia-funded programme actively paying them to start companies. Supercell and Rovio proved it at scale, their alumni and capital seeded the next generation, and Slush grew out of the student community in the middle of it. Finns are ambivalent about the Nokia story rather than triumphant about it — ask someone what they think, and you'll get a much more interesting answer than any article gives you.

🌲
Juhannus (Midsummer) is the shutdown to know about. Finns leave the city en masse for summer cottages and Helsinki becomes genuinely empty — shops, restaurants and museums close from Midsummer Eve afternoon through Midsummer Day, with holiday transit schedules. It falls on the Friday and Saturday between 19 and 25 June. If you get invited to a cottage, cancel whatever else you had planned and go. If you don't, the city is a ghost town.
🧊
If you're here November to March, buy ice grips. The slip-on kind, €10–20 at any pharmacy or Clas Ohlson. Pavements get genuinely treacherous and this is the least glamorous, most useful purchase you will make. Indoors is heated to about 21°C everywhere, so dress in layers you can strip.

🏰There Are Two Tallinns

One day is enough to do both justice if you are disciplined about timing. The mistake everyone makes is letting the Old Town eat the whole day.

Old Town (Vanalinn)
Medieval · UNESCO · Tourism hub
The best-preserved medieval city centre in Northern Europe — 1.9 km of original wall and 20 defensive towers. Two parts: Toompea up the hill (nobility, cathedrals, the viewing platforms) and the Lower Town below (merchants, guildhalls, Town Hall Square). Go early for silence and magic, and be done by noon before the tour groups land. Evening is also spectacular.
Telliskivi Creative City
Local · Art · Food · Bars
A former Soviet rail-industrial complex, now around 200 creative businesses: murals, food stalls, coffee labs, design shops, Fotografiska, the Balti Jaama Market. Twenty minutes' walk from Old Town, or tram 1/2/5. This is where Estonians actually hang out.

Fares

Highly dynamic. Advance, midweek, off-peak runs €12–22 one way. Walk-up, weekend or summer can hit €40–60. Midweek sailings are 30–40% cheaper than weekends — if your schedule is flexible, this is where the savings are. Book direct on the operator's site or app; aggregators add fees.

How far ahead

Off-season midweek, a few days is fine and you can often walk up. June through August, any weekend, and around holidays: book two to four weeks ahead. Friday evening out and Sunday evening back genuinely sell out.

Getting to the terminal

West Terminal 2 (Tyynenmerenkatu 14, Jätkäsaari) is trams 7 and 9 from Kaivokatu by the Central Station — six to nine minutes on a normal AB ticket. Don't confuse West Terminal 1 and 2; they're a few hundred metres apart and both in Jätkäsaari. Tallink and Eckerö use T2. Katajanokka is tram 4 or 5, or a 20-minute walk from Senate Square.

🛳
On board it's card-only, and cafeteria meals run €20–28 — bring food from a supermarket instead. Expect trolleys of cheap alcohol being wheeled back to Finland; that's what half the boat is there for.

Terminal to Old Town is a flat, signposted 15-minute walk — that's what most people should do. Tram 2 also runs it. For taxis use Bolt, which is an Estonian company founded in Tallinn; street taxis at the port overcharge badly.

Cash is not accepted on public transport at all — contactless card at the validator, up to six tickets on one card so you can cover a group.

🏰Old Town Must-Sees
  • Town Hall Square (Raekoja plats) — the medieval heart. Morning coffee here before the crowds is the whole point.
  • Toompea Hill — walk up for Alexander Nevsky Cathedral, Toompea Castle (now parliament), and the view.
  • Kohtuotsa and Patkuli platforms — two of the best city overlooks in the Baltics. Go at sunset.
  • St. Olaf's Church Tower — Tallinn's tallest church. Climb it for 360° views, €5.
  • Viru Gate and the medieval walls — walking the old walls is free and extraordinary. Don't skip it.
  • Café Maiasmokk — opened 1864, the oldest café in Estonia. Coffee, marzipan, pastries, and a living piece of history.
🎫
Skip the Tallinn Card on a day trip. At €29 for 24 hours you'd need three or more paid museums to break even, and the clock starts on first use.
🎨Telliskivi
  • Fotografiska Tallinn — world-class rotating photography museum, around €15–18. The Michelin-noted restaurant on site is accessible without a museum ticket.
  • Balti Jaama Turg — restored three-storey market next door. Produce, cheeses, pickles, street food, and a superb vintage floor. Lunch for €8–10.
  • F-Hoone — the anchor restaurant. Industrial-chic, diverse menu, affordable, good for a long relaxed meal.
  • Depoo — food street built from old train cars and shipping containers. Packed on weekends with live music.
  • Põhjala Brewery Taproom — Estonia's best-known craft brewery, ten to fourteen taps.
  • Saturday flea market, 10:00–15:00 — Soviet ephemera, vintage clothing, crafts. Bring some cash. If you can pick your day, pick Saturday.
  • The street art — murals cover the whole complex and are constantly repainted. Spend thirty minutes just walking and looking.

Kalamaja, the wooden-house district between Old Town and Telliskivi, is worth walking through rather than around — pastel timber houses, quiet streets, cafés.

🍽Estonian Food
Black rye bread
Dense, tangy, slightly sweet. The Estonian food baseline, served with everything, and vastly underrated by the world.
Smoked fish
Baltic herring, salmon and sprats, eaten with rye bread and sour cream. Get it at Balti Jaama market.
Verivorst (blood sausage)
National comfort food, traditionally Christmas but available year-round. More nuanced than it sounds and worth trying once.
Olde Hansa
Touristy? Yes. But the medieval atmosphere is genuinely extraordinary — elk stew, costumed staff, the whole production. Worth it once.
💶
Eat in Telliskivi, not Old Town. An Old Town dinner runs €35–55 and you are paying for the location. The same money goes twice as far ten minutes away.
🧭Estonian Culture
  • Soviet occupation ended in 1991. Many adults you'll meet lived it. This shapes everything — the reserve, the pragmatism, the fierce pride in independence.
  • The Singing Revolution. Estonia won independence partly through mass gatherings from 1987 to 1991 where hundreds of thousands of people sang banned national songs. Estonians take choral tradition seriously, and you should too.
  • It is the most digital society on earth. 99% of public services are online. Digital signatures have full legal force. Internet voting has been binding in national elections since 2005. The state is legally forbidden from asking you for information it already holds. Don't be surprised that everything runs on an app.
  • Estonian is related to Finnish, not to any Scandinavian language. English works fine everywhere you'll be.

🛂EU Border Rules — Read This One

EES is already live. The EU Entry/Exit System has been fully operational since April 2026. On your first Schengen entry you'll be sent to a booth for a facial image and fingerprints, creating a digital record that replaces passport stamping. No application, no fee — but expect longer queues at Helsinki-Vantaa on arrival, so build in extra time on the inbound leg.

ETIAS is coming but is not required yet. A €20 pre-travel authorisation for Americans, valid three years or until your passport expires. Launch is expected in the last quarter of 2026, followed by a grace period of roughly six months. Sources disagree on when it becomes strictly mandatory — somewhere between spring and autumn 2027.

⚠️
The ETIAS application portal is not open. Any website currently accepting ETIAS applications is fraudulent — this is an active scam targeting exactly the people reading this. The only official source is travel-europe.europa.eu/etias_en. Check it six to eight weeks before you fly.
🇪🇪
The Tallinn day trip is intra-Schengen, so there's no routine passport control and EES doesn't re-trigger. Carry your passport anyway: ferry operators require photo ID to board, a US driver's licence is not sufficient for non-EU citizens, and spot checks by border guards do happen.
📱Phone & Data
Travel eSIM — best for 4+ days
Airalo, Nomad, eSIMPal. Roughly 5 GB / 30 days for $12.50, or 10 GB / 60 days around $10. Install before you fly; it activates on landing.
US carrier day pass — best for 1–3 days
T-Mobile $5/day, AT&T and Verizon $10/day. A ten-day trip is ~$100 on AT&T versus $20–40 on an eSIM, so the maths turns fast.
Local prepaid SIM — best for a semester
DNA at any R-Kioski: SIM €4.90 with €5 credit, then €0.99/day unlimited or €24.90/month unlimited. No ID required. Elisa and Telia are equivalent. This is the obvious answer if you're here more than two weeks.

Coverage is excellent — near-100 Mbps throughout Helsinki, including indoors and on the metro. One catch: Finnish domestic plans cap EU roaming data, so a DNA SIM won't give you unlimited data in Estonia. Check the fair-use allowance before relying on it for the Tallinn trip.

💳Money
  • A contactless card is your most important item. Visa or Mastercard, tap-enabled. Apple Pay and Google Pay work essentially everywhere.
  • Carry €40–60 in small notes as a fallback. You may not spend it. Flea markets and second-hand stalls are the main cash holdouts.
  • Watch for "Ei käteistä" signs — that means cash not accepted, which is now more common than cash-only.
  • 1- and 2-cent coins aren't circulated; cash totals round to the nearest five cents.
  • ATMs are branded Otto or Nosto. Use a card with no foreign transaction fee — over a semester this is real money.
  • Tax-free shopping applies to non-EU visitors: minimum €40 per store per day, goods unused and sealed. Finnish VAT is 25.5%, so you recover roughly 10–16% net. Get the Customs stamp before you leave — it cannot be obtained retroactively.
😴Beating Jet Lag

Helsinki runs +7 hours on US Eastern, +8 on Central, +9 on Mountain and +10 on Pacific. This protocol works, and skipping the pre-departure part is why most people lose their first three days.

3–5 days before you fly

  • Shift your sleep one hour earlier each night
  • Get morning sunlight within 30 minutes of waking
  • Move caffeine to noon or earlier
  • Drink more water than feels necessary
  • Exercise in the morning, not the evening
  • Eat light in the 24 hours before travel

On the flight

  • Reset your watch to Helsinki time immediately
  • Sleep during Helsinki's nighttime, with an eye mask
  • Avoid screens before your sleep window
  • Drink water constantly; no caffeine in sleep periods
  • Walk the aisles
  • Eat on destination meal times
  • Low-dose melatonin (0.5–1mg) 30 min before sleeping

The five non-negotiables

  • Shift sleep one hour earlier nightly for three days or more before departure.
  • Sleep on the plane during Helsinki's nighttime hours, with an eye mask.
  • Go outside into natural light immediately on arrival.
  • Do not nap on arrival day. Push through to 9 or 10pm local time. This is the one that decides everything.
  • Use an eye mask every night — in summer it never gets dark, and in winter you'll want the sleep.

Expect a wall around 3pm on days one and two. Plan something active for it rather than something restful.

🎒What to Pack

Finns dress casually and functionally. Nobody will judge your clothes; the weather will judge your shoes.

SeasonThe essentials
Nov–MarReal winter coat, thermal base layer, hat, gloves, scarf, waterproof boots with grip plus slip-on ice grips. Indoors is 21°C everywhere, so layers you can strip.
Apr & OctWaterproof shell, layers, waterproof shoes. The rain comes sideways.
May–SepLayers plus a light rain jacket. Even July evenings drop to 12–14°C and the sea breeze is cold — bring a sweater regardless of forecast.

Bring

  • Contactless card — your single most important item
  • Eye mask, every trip, every season
  • Passport (ferry ID and bar entry)
  • Light daypack, 20–25L
  • EU plug adapter (Type C/F)
  • Power bank
  • Reusable water bottle
  • Quick-dry towel for saunas
  • Swimsuit — for mixed saunas and Allas
  • Waterproof walking shoes for cobblestones and trails

Leave behind

  • An umbrella — wind makes them useless; a hood is better
  • Cash beyond a small reserve
  • A heavy winter coat if you're here May–September
  • Dressy clothes — almost nowhere requires them
  • Bottled water
📲Apps to Download Before You Land
  • HSL — Helsinki transit tickets and the journey planner. The single most useful one.
  • 112 Suomi — emergency calls that transmit your exact GPS position to the dispatcher.
  • Bolt — rideshare in both Helsinki and Tallinn, and an Estonian company.
  • Wolt — food delivery, Finnish, and everywhere.
  • Google Translate — the camera translation is what you'll actually use, for supermarket labels.
  • Your ferry operator's app — mobile boarding passes work fine.
🧠Living Well While You're Here
  • Unpack properly rather than living out of a suitcase, even for a short stay. Designate one charging spot, one place for shoes, one place for documents.
  • Protect seven to eight hours of sleep. The light, the excitement and the late nights compound fast, and in June the sun is a liar.
  • Take the occasional quiet evening. It makes the next social day better, and nobody is keeping score.
  • November is hard for everyone here, locals included. Daylight lamps, exercise and plans with other people are the standard Finnish countermeasures. If you're low, that's the season, not you.
  • Say yes to the sauna, the cottage, and the Midsummer invitation. These are not small-talk offers. Finns don't invite casually, and the invitation is the relationship.
  • The best memories won't be in your camera roll. Be in the conversation rather than photographing it.
